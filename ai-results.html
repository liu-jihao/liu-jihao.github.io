AI-generated results not verified by a human

Papers obtained with the Danus system that have been checked by machines but not yet by a human. The papers are listed below, after an explanation of why this page exists and how I handle credit and corrections.

Why this page exists

I think of myself as a mathematician, and as a mathematician I still believe, in a certain sense, that a paper should be verified by a human before it is posted to arXiv. However, revising a paper carefully takes a long time, and in the meantime the credit for the result may be lost, both for me and for our system, Danus.

Consider the following situation. I use AI to produce a paper whose result is important. What should I do?

  1. I post the paper to arXiv. Then I am criticized for not having verified the paper in detail. This goes against my values as a mathematician.
  2. I do not post the paper anywhere. But what if someone else obtains the result and posts it before me? In that case I lose the credit. Even if I use a Bitcoin timestamp, the credit for the result has to be shared with others.

This is a dilemma. I would also like to say a word to those who are not yet aware of how reliable AI has become: things have changed significantly in the past few months. A good AI system may now verify a paper better than a human could, even without Lean verification. Because of this, there are, and there will be, a great many proofs that are “not human-verified, not Lean-verified, but (essentially) correct”.

So here is what I plan to do. On this page I will post some of my papers that have not been verified by a human (but have been verified by machines). I believe that the results posted here are important enough to attract the attention of experts.

For example, the first papers posted here include Fujita’s base-point-freeness conjecture in dimensions 6 and 7 (my own research field before all this AI work; to be posted shortly) and the existence of a non-hyperlinear group. I believe these results are of enough interest to experts in the related fields that they would prefer to see proofs not verified by a human than to see no proofs at all. Besides, even if the papers are not so well written, experts can use their own AI to digest and understand the proofs, and use them to obtain stronger results. In particular, I would be happy to see a proof of Fujita’s base-point-freeness conjecture in all dimensions at this point.

Credit and corrections

  1. Sharing credit. For any paper posted here, provided that the paper is correct and has no fatal mistake, I will be happy to share credit with any person or organization who obtained the same or stronger results, by whatever method, before I posted the paper. Here “fatal mistake” is meant in the traditional sense: in particular, bad writing does not count, and neither do mistakes that do not affect the main results of the paper, for example in the roll call of references. The result may be written in Lean or in natural language. Please be aware that I will ask for a timestamp. A nearly finished draft does not count, because AI can find my result and generate the rest of the paper within hours. If you cannot show me a timestamp, I will not grant you credit.
  2. Fatal gaps and minor mistakes. I will be happy if anyone points out a fatal gap in a paper posted here. For the record, one fatal mistake was pointed out in May 2026, when I was using a very embryonic version of Danus; that paper was corrected before it was submitted to arXiv. Since then, no one has pointed out a fatal mistake in any of my papers. If you point out a fatal gap in one of these papers and fix it, I will be very happy to collaborate. Anyone who points out minor mistakes or typos will be acknowledged when the paper is posted to arXiv or submitted.
  3. Credit to other people’s work. I am aware that AI is bad at giving credit to other people and to their results. Things like “someone did 95% of the work and AI did the last 5%” appear every day. Because of the limitations of generative AI and of our writing system, I cannot guarantee proper attribution everywhere in these papers either. But, as I explained above, this should not prevent these papers from being posted: they are expected to be of enough interest to experts in the related fields that a proof not verified by a human is better than no proof at all. In any case, if you are a human author of a related article and feel that your paper is not given enough credit in my writing, please let me know. I will update the paper before submitting it to arXiv, and I will acknowledge you.
  4. Credit to humans or to AI systems. Some argue that the credit for mathematical work of this kind should go not to the person who wrote the prompt but to the AI system. I do not give my personal opinion on this argument here, but I would like to point out that all the works on this page were obtained by the Danus system, and I am one of the developers of Danus: I am the first author of the report on the Danus system. So even if you think that the credit should go to the AI system, it should go to the Danus system (the Rethlas/Danus team), and hence I still have the credit.

2. Nonhyperlinear groups exist
Jihao Liu
AI-generated paper · obtained by Danus on 19 September 2026 · posted here 20 September 2026 · not yet submitted to arXiv

This paper constructs a countable discrete group that is not hyperlinear. Equivalently, its group von Neumann algebra, with its canonical trace, does not embed into an ultrapower of the hyperfinite II1 factor, so Connes’ embedding problem has a negative answer for groups. The group is the generalized wreath product W = (⊕G/Γ Z/2) ⋊ G, where Γ = EL3(F2[x1, x2, x3]) and G = EL3(F2[x1±1, x2±1, x3±1]) ⋊ SL3(Z). It is one of the groups that Kun and Thom (arXiv:2608.06222) proved to be nonsofic, building on the first nonsofic group, found by OpenAI.

The new ingredients are two theorems on tracial ultraproducts of matrix algebras. First, the relative commutant of a finitely generated Kazhdan group in such an ultraproduct is itself an ultraproduct of finite-dimensional subalgebras of the same matrix algebras; this answers Open Problem 6.2(a) of Alekseev and Thom (arXiv:2608.05362). Second, if a Kazhdan group is generated by a subgroup together with finitely many elements that conjugate this subgroup into itself, then the group normalizes the relative commutant of the subgroup. Danus ran with seven proof workers and found the proof in 7 hours and 11 minutes.
